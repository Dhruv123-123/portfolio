Rubik's cube

Speedcubing · since 2016

Background

Someone gave me a Rubik's cube for my tenth birthday, presumably as a joke, and I spent the next four days on a YouTube tutorial before my first solve. It took a little over four minutes and I was so proud that I made everyone in the house watch it twice. That was the beginner's method, layer by layer, and for about a year I thought that was all there was.

Then I found out about CFOP, the method actual speedcubers use, and that the world record at the time was under five seconds. Four minutes to five seconds is not a gap you close by trying harder. It's a gap you close by learning a very large number of algorithms.

Learning full OLL and PLL

CFOP ends with two stages: orienting the last layer (57 cases) and permuting it (21 cases). Most people learn a handful of each and a workaround for the rest. I decided to learn all 78, because I was thirteen and it seemed like the kind of thing a person could just decide.

I made flashcards. I drilled them on the school bus, on a cube with the stickers half peeled off, until the algorithms lived in my hands instead of my head. My times went from sub-30 to sub-20 to, eventually, a sub-18 average on 3x3, which is where I've stayed for a few years now. On 2x2 I average under three seconds, which mostly involves memorizing a different set of algorithms and having very fast fingers.

Blindfolded

Blindfolded solving is a completely different sport that happens to use the same object. You look at the scrambled cube for as long as you need, memorize where every piece has to go as a string of letters, put on the blindfold, and then execute a sequence of moves that swaps pieces two at a time while you recite the letters in your head.

I learned the Old Pochmann method one summer because I was bored, and the first time it actually worked I took the blindfold off, saw a solved cube, and sat there for a full minute not entirely believing it. I am not fast at it. I am, however, capable of doing it at a party, which turns out to be worth more.

The world record

On January 26, 2020, seven of us stood in Mantri Square Mall in Bengaluru and solved 736 Rubik's cubes in one hour, which became a world record for the most cubes solved by a team of kids in an hour.

The logistics were the hard part. You need a scrambling crew that can stay ahead of seven solvers who are each finishing a cube every thirty seconds. You need a rhythm so nobody reaches for the same cube. You need to figure out, in advance, what happens when your hands start cramping at minute forty, because they will. Mine did. I kept going with what felt like two frozen claws and I don't remember much of the last fifteen minutes except the counting.

The videos

There are videos of most of this. There is footage of the record attempt, of me at cubing competitions with braces and a haircut I did not consent to, and of at least one blindfolded solve where I very confidently take the blindfold off to reveal a cube that is not solved.

They are too embarrassing to show. This is not negotiable.

Conclusion

There's a cube on my desk at Terranox. I solve it while things compile, and I've gotten good at not looking at it while I do, which unsettles people in a way I enjoy. I still average sub-18. I'd like to get sub-15 one day, but that requires learning a new set of algorithms and I'm no longer thirteen.
