F1 and sim racing

Sim racing · league racing since 2020

Background

I got into Formula 1 the way a lot of people my age did: a friend put on Drive to Survive, I rolled my eyes, and three weeks later I was setting alarms for practice sessions. That was the 2020 season, the one that ran through empty grandstands. Growing up in Bengaluru the European races landed at a civilized 6:30pm, so Sunday evenings became the one fixed point in a year where nothing else was fixed.

Watching wasn't enough for long. I wanted to know what it actually felt like to hit the brakes at 300 km/h and trust that the car would stop, and short of a budget of several hundred million dollars, sim racing was the closest I was going to get. I started in F1 2020 on a controller, which is roughly like learning piano on a touchscreen. Within two months I had talked my parents into a second-hand Logitech G29 wheel, clamped to a desk that was never designed for it.

The setup

The wheel changed everything, but the single biggest jump in my lap times came from something far less glamorous: a load cell brake pedal. A normal pedal measures how far you press it. A load cell measures how hard. It turns out your leg remembers pressure much better than it remembers distance, and the day I installed one I found half a second at every circuit I knew.

The rig grew the way these things do. An aluminum profile frame replaced the desk clamp after the desk lost. A second monitor turned into three. I still haven't bought a direct-drive wheelbase, which is the sim racing equivalent of saying you haven't bought a house yet: everyone assumes it's coming.

League racing

Public lobbies are chaos. Someone will always brake-check you into turn one and then rage-quit. So about six months in I joined an online league: a fixed grid of the same thirty drivers every week, proper qualifying sessions, stewards who reviewed incidents and handed out penalties, and a championship that ran for a full season with a points table that people cared about far more than was healthy.

The race I still think about was at Suzuka. I qualified fourth, made a bad tire call, and spent the last twenty laps on rubber that had nothing left in it with a faster car about a second behind me. I held him off. Every lap I could see him fill my mirrors through 130R and every lap I placed the car exactly where he couldn't go. I finished third. It's the closest I've come to understanding why real drivers describe a defensive stint as the most satisfying thing they do.

Top 800

In ranked play I climbed to the top 800 in the world, which sounds better than it felt at the time, because the top 800 is where you start meeting people who are genuinely, unreasonably fast. What separated them from me was never raw pace. It was consistency. A fast lap is worth nothing if you can't do it forty times in a row, and the drivers above me simply made fewer mistakes than I did over a race distance.

That realization changed how I practiced. Instead of chasing a purple sector, I started recording telemetry and laying my traces over faster drivers' laps: throttle, brake, steering, speed. You can see exactly where you're losing time, and it is almost never where you think. I was braking too early into slow corners and too late into fast ones, for a year, without knowing.

Conclusion

I still race most weekends and still watch every Grand Prix. The league has slowed down as everyone got jobs, but the Discord is alive and the grudges are intact. The thing I'd like to do next is take it off the screen: a proper karting championship, or a single track day in a real car, to find out how much of what I learned actually transfers. My guess is the braking does and the courage doesn't.
